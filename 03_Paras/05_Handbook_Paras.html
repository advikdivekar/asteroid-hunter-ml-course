Paras: labels, dataset building and the classical ML model

Marks this part carries: Model Development (4, shared with Satvik) and the model-ready data part of Data Preprocessing (3, shared with Aaryan). Why it matters: this is the stage-1 machine-learning model, the one that decides for every blob whether it is real. It is also where "garbage labels in, garbage model out" is most visible, and where the course's classical topics meet: feature engineering, SVM, trees and boosting, PCA and k-means.

Classical ML only. Our faculty asked for classical machine learning. The deployed model (bundle v6) is XGBoost on 29 measured features plus 20 PCA numbers. The CNN we built first is kept only as a comparison: it tied (AP 0.898 vs 0.902), so the simpler, explainable model is the one we use. Lesson: project/docs/classical_realbogus.md.

Pitfalls

  • Saying "the model is 99% accurate": accuracy is meaningless at 3% positives; use AP and false alarms at fixed recall.
  • Forgetting that training is balanced (16.7% real) while the real sky is about 3%: raw probabilities are inflated.
  • Calling labels "ground truth": they are "best available", with known noise.
  • Using a feature that only exists for planted fakes (true magnitude, true speed): that is target leakage. Every feature here is measured on the stamp.
  • Choosing settings on the test night. Everything was chosen on validation.

The 29 hand-made features (features/compute_stamp_features.py), each a question a person would ask the three pictures:

  • 6 from the blob finder: snr, peak, npix, a, b, flux.
  • brightness at the centre and peak in science, reference and difference, and in a ring around the blob (8).
  • old vs new: ref_to_sci (was it already in the old picture?), diff_to_sci (how much of tonight's light is new?).
  • shape: diff_psf_match (correlation with a round star of ZTF's blur: the most important input), sharpness (cosmic rays are one hot pixel), concentration, size, elongation, offset from centre, symmetry, roughness.
  • bad subtraction: darkest pixel, share of very dark pixels, diff_dipole (dark hole next to the bright spot); ref_psf_match (a star in the old picture).

PCA (models/train_realbogus_trees.py): the central 15 x 15 pixels of all three channels (675 numbers) projected onto the 20 directions in which training stamps vary most; they keep 78% of the variance. Unsupervised: PCA never sees a label.

XGBoost (600 trees, depth 4, learning rate 0.05, 80% of rows and columns per tree): each tree asks up to 4 yes/no questions such as "is diff_psf_match below 0.46?" and adds a small vote; the sum through a sigmoid is p(real). Boosting = each new tree is fitted to the errors of the trees so far. Settings chosen on the validation nights only (depth 4 beat 5 and 6).

1. Labels: where they come from and how good they are

There is no list of "all asteroids in this image". There is a list of things we put there ourselves, and a catalogue of things others found.

1.1 Injection-recovery (planted fakes) -> perfect labels

  • plan_injections: for each of 300 fakes per night: magnitude uniform 17-22, speed uniform 5-100 arcsec/hour, random direction, random start; positions follow the real frame times on frame 1's sky grid, then are converted through the sky to each frame's own pixels (after we found pointing shifts ignored the old way).
  • render_point_source: the fake is drawn from ZTF's own PSF of that frame (not a textbook Moffat), shifted by a sub-pixel amount with cubic interpolation and rescaled so the total flux is exact (otherwise every fake would sit on a pixel centre and a model could learn "perfectly centred = fake").
  • inject_point_source: Poisson noise (Poisson(stamp x gain) / gain), added to both science and difference, so reference = science - difference is unchanged: exactly the situation of a new object.
  • Weakness: fakes are only as realistic as the simulation. Any difference is a distribution shift the model may exploit. So: real PSF, real noise, real times, correct sky motion, and a realism test (1.5).
  • Magnitudes are drawn uniformly, not like real asteroids (which are mostly faint), so every brightness bin has enough fakes to measure completeness. Remember this when quoting rates.

1.2 Known asteroids -> real labels

Positions of catalogued asteroids at each frame time. We match detections to positions within 2 px with a KD-tree (the nearest neighbour in about log n steps instead of comparing 7,700 x 300 pairs per frame). We use only known asteroids brighter than V = 20.5: fainter ones are mostly chance matches. The check: P(random point lands within 2 px of some detection) = detections x pi x 2^2 / good-pixel area = 1.15%; 114 known asteroids x 1.15% = 1.3 expected chance matches per frame, which is what we saw, so the early faint "matches" were mostly wrong. Observer location matters: SkyBoT defaults to the centre of the Earth; ZTF is about 6,400 km away, shifting an asteroid 1 AU away by up to about 9 arcsec (4 pixels). We pass location="I41" (ZTF's observatory code).

1.3 Everything else = bogus

Includes variable stars, artifacts, and the rare real object nobody has catalogued: label noise we accept. Consequence: a "false positive" on real data may be a real discovery, which is exactly what the project hunts. Class imbalance: one training night: 1,059 real vs 36,161 bogus (1 : 34).

1.4 The label-quality story (two bugs, both found by looking at where the model disagreed with the labels)

  1. SkyBoT offset. The CNN's most confident "bogus" detections were clean new point sources and every one was a known asteroid (V 17-20). SkyBoT's predictions sat about 2.9 arcsec from our detections in a constant direction (-2.4, +1.5 px) on every night, with scatter of only 0.1-0.5 px. Our WCS matches Gaia stars to 0.04 px and JPL Horizons matches our detections to 0.1-0.3 arcsec, so the error is in SkyBoT. First fix: a 4-px match radius (AP 0.877 -> 0.894). Better fix (label-quality phase): use JPL Horizons positions where available (median error 0.26 px vs 2.72 px, 314 matches over 12 nights) and a 2-px radius (4x less area, 4x fewer chance matches); SkyBoT only as fallback, tagged by source so a detected faint known asteroid is never mistaken for a discovery.
  2. Fakes moved straight in pixel coordinates, ignoring the telescope's pointing shifts, so real asteroids failed to link while fakes did. Fix: plan fakes on frame 1's sky grid and convert. Lessons: check the direction of offsets, not only the size (a systematic error hid behind chance coincidences); a model's most confident "mistakes" are the cheapest way to find label errors; test your simulation against real objects, not only against itself.

1.5 Do the fakes look real? (adversarial validation)

Train a classifier to separate fakes from real known asteroids. AUC 0.5 = cannot. Rules: match the confounders (sample fakes with the same magnitude distribution as the real asteroids) and cross-validate by night. Result: AUC 0.46-0.48 (two seeds: 0.481 and 0.463; report 0.47 +/- 0.02, not three decimals of false precision). The same trick detects train/test distribution shift in general.

2. Building the dataset

build_night_detections per unit: load frames -> plant fakes -> detect -> label -> describe (RA/Dec, common-grid x/y, magnitude, seeing) -> cut stamps -> save detections__<unit>.parquet, stamps__<unit>.npy (float16), injections__<unit>.parquet. Built once, re-used many times (about 40 s per night).

  • Save everything, choose later: every detection's stamp is kept (about 230 MB per night as float16); balancing happens when assembling, so the same files serve training (balanced), validation (realistic) and the real search (all).
  • Seeds: each night's generator is seeded with [project seed, night]: repeatable (an early draft used Python's hash, which changes every run).
  • A float16 bug: saturated star cores exceed 65,504 (float16's maximum) and became inf; fix: clip before converting.
  • Sizes: 11 nights; per night 32-41k detections, 1,053-1,199 real, 27-37 bogus per real.

2.1 Stamps (the CNN's input)

Three channels of 31 x 31 pixels around the blob: science, reference, difference. Why three? A real new object: blob in science and difference, nothing in reference. Bad subtraction: bright star in both science and reference, dipole in difference. Cosmic ray: sharp spike in science and difference, narrower than a star. Noise: faint speck. Masked edge: step in all. A person compares panels; the CNN learns to. 31 px because the blur is about 2 px wide and 15 px of surroundings is enough to see a neighbouring star or an edge (ZTF's own classifier uses 63 x 63).

2.2 Normalisation, augmentation, balancing

  • Normalise per stamp and channel: (value - median) / (1.4826 x MAD), clipped to [-10, 50]. Median (not mean) so a star barely moves it; MAD x 1.4826 equals the standard deviation for Gaussian noise but ignores outliers; "5" now means 5 sigma on any night; clipping stops a saturated star dominating. Nothing is fitted on training data, so there is no leakage here (contrast StandardScaler).
  • Augment (training only): rotations by 0/90/180/270 degrees and optional mirror = 8 versions of every stamp; the sky has no "up". Acts like more data, teaches orientation does not matter. Never augment validation or test.
  • Balance training: all real rows + 5 bogus per real (8,863 real -> 53,178 stamps, 16.7% real) because with 3% positives the gradient is dominated by easy bogus examples and "always bogus" is a comfortable optimum. Cost: inflated probabilities (the model believes real is 16.7% common). Validation stays realistic (67,041 detections, 3.2% real) so precision and false-alarm numbers are honest.

3. Neural-network theory (the from-scratch notebook)

  • Neuron: output = activation(w.x + b); with a sigmoid it is logistic regression.
  • Why stack: with ReLU (max(0,z)) each hidden neuron contributes a "bent line"; 16 bent lines make a curved boundary (the half-moons: logistic regression 0.88, network 0.94). Without a non-linear activation, stacked layers collapse to one linear function.
  • Forward pass: z1 = X W1 + b1; h = ReLU(z1); z2 = h W2 + b2; p = sigmoid(z2).
  • Loss: binary cross-entropy L = -mean(y log p + (1-y) log(1-p)). Confident and wrong costs a lot (-log 0.01 = 4.6); confident and right costs almost nothing (-log 0.99 = 0.01).
  • Backpropagation = chain rule from the output back. Output: for sigmoid + cross-entropy, dL/dz2 = p - y (the sigmoid's slope cancels the log's slope). Output weights: dL/dW2 = h^T (p - y) / n. Back into the hidden layer: error_hidden = (p - y) W2^T x ReLU'(z1) where ReLU' is 1 if z1 > 0 else 0. Hidden weights: dL/dW1 = X^T error_hidden / n. Each layer reuses the one after it: one backward sweep gives every gradient.
  • Gradient check: nudge one weight by epsilon = 1e-5 on both sides: (L(w+e) - L(w-e)) / 2e must equal the backprop gradient (ours: -0.02896520 both).
  • Gradient descent: w <- w - lr x gradient. Too small: slow; too large (try 5.0): overshoots and diverges. Adam adapts each weight's step using its recent gradients.
  • Initialisation: small and random; if all weights started equal every neuron would stay identical.
  • PyTorch's four steps: optimizer.zero_grad(), loss = loss_fn(model(X), y), loss.backward(), optimizer.step(). BCEWithLogitsLoss fuses sigmoid and cross-entropy (stable), so the model outputs raw logits; torch.no_grad() and model.eval() when scoring.
  • Overfitting demo: a 256 x 256 network on 40 points: training loss falls toward 1e-5 (memorising noise) while validation loss is lowest at step 22 then climbs: hence early stopping.

4. The CNN in detail

4.1 Architecture (define_realbogus_cnn.py; see 07_Diagrams/03_cnn_architecture.svg)

Input (3, 31, 31) -> [Conv3x3 16 + ReLU] -> [Conv3x3 16 + ReLU] -> MaxPool (31->15) -> [Conv3x3 32 + ReLU] -> [Conv3x3 32 + ReLU] -> MaxPool (15->7) -> [Conv3x3 64 + ReLU] -> MaxPool (7->3) -> Flatten (64 x 3 x 3 = 576) -> Dropout 0.3 -> Dense 64 + ReLU -> Dense 1 -> (sigmoid at scoring time).

  • Convolution: a 3x3 filter slides over the stamp; at each position it multiplies 9 pixels by 9 learned weights and sums. With 3 input channels each filter has 3x3x3 weights and sees science, reference and difference together, which is how it learns "bright in science and difference, nothing in reference". Padding 1 keeps the size.
  • Pooling: max-pool halves the size, so deeper filters see larger areas (edges and blobs first, then "point source", "dipole", "streak").
  • Dropout 0.3: randomly switches off 30% of neurons while training so no neuron is relied on.

4.2 Counting the 72,145 weights (do this yourself)

params = (in_channels x 3 x 3 + 1) x out_channels for a conv layer: conv1 (3->16) = 448; conv2 (16->16) = 2,320; conv3 (16->32) = 4,640; conv4 (32->32) = 9,248; conv5 (32->64) = 18,496; dense (576 -> 64) = 576 x 64 + 64 = 36,928; output (64 -> 1) = 65. Sum = 72,145.

4.3 Training recipe (train_realbogus_cnn.py, configs/train.yaml)

setting value why
loss BCEWithLogitsLoss stable cross-entropy
optimiser Adam, lr 0.001 robust default
batch 256 stable gradients, fits easily
augmentation random 90-degree rotations + mirror sky has no up
early stopping patience 4 on validation AP keep the best epoch
Judged by validation AP on realistic data, not training loss (training loss 0.08 vs validation 0.035 can both be right: training is balanced and augmented, validation is easier). The final run used all 25 epochs; best validation AP (0.899) at epoch 21.

4.4 Results (validation, 67,041 detections, 3.2% real)

model AP precision at 90% recall false positives per frame
logistic regression, 6 features 0.780 0.191 813
CNN 0.899 0.522 176
At the same completeness the CNN cuts false alarms 4.6x; on the untouched test night AP 0.938. The completeness-vs-magnitude curves look alike at 90% recall because we fixed recall: the CNN's gain is purity, which makes linking cheap: after the CNN filter only about 24% of detections remain, and seed pairs drop from 52k to 3.5k.

4.5 Cross-fitting (stacking without leakage; 07_Diagrams/05_cross_fitting.svg)

8 training nights -> 4 folds of 2 nights. For each fold train a CNN on the other 6 nights and score the 2 held-out nights; validation and test nights are scored by the final CNN. Without it, the tracklet model would see over-confident CNN scores on training nights and learn "score_min is always high for real things", which would not hold on new nights.

Going backwards, the error at the output is simply p minus y for sigmoid with cross-entropy. That error times the transpose of W2, times 1 where the hidden neuron was active and 0 where it was not, gives the error at the hidden layer. Each weight's gradient is its input times the error arriving at it. Then gradient descent moves each weight a small step against its gradient. We wrote this by hand in numpy and checked it against a numerical gradient to eight decimals."

Paras: viva questions and model answers

0. The deployed classical model (read first)

Q. Why classical ML and not the CNN? Our faculty asked for classical ML, and the evidence agrees: on the same data XGBoost + PCA scored 0.902 validation AP and the CNN 0.898; on the whole pipeline both find the same known asteroids. A tie goes to the simpler, faster, explainable model.

Q. What are the 49 inputs? 29 hand-made features (6 from the blob finder, 23 measured on the three stamps: centre brightness, "already in the old picture?", star-shape correlation, sharpness, dark hole, size, elongation, symmetry...) and 20 PCA numbers of the central pixels.

Q. Why does PCA help if it never sees the labels? It finds the main ways stamps differ (a bright centre, a dipole, an edge); real and junk stamps sit in different places along those directions, so the trees can use them. It also adds information our hand-made features missed.

Q. Why did logistic regression and the SVM drop on the test night (0.655, 0.877) while XGBoost held (0.938)? The test night is sharper and deeper than training (distribution shift). Linear and kernel boundaries extrapolate to feature values they never saw; trees treat values beyond the last split like the last split, so they degrade gracefully.

Q. Which input matters most and why? diff_psf_match, the correlation of the difference patch with a round star of ZTF's blur: a real point source is star-shaped, cosmic rays are too sharp, dipoles and edges are not round.

Q. How were the settings chosen? On validation only: depth 4 (0.9034) beat 5 and 6 (0.9025); adding PCA raised AP by about 0.005; training on all 305,463 blobs instead of the balanced 53,178 did not help.

Q. What does k-means add? It is unsupervised: without labels it groups the 44,290 junk stamps into kinds (93% faint noise, star leftovers, bright stars, old-picture-only). That tells us what junk to design features for; a classifier only says real or not.

Q. Is p(real) a true probability? No: training is 16.7% real, the sky 3%, so scores are inflated; we only use them to rank and cut, and Satvik's base-rate adjustment converts them if needed.

A. Labels and data preparation

1. Where do your labels come from? Three sources: planted fake asteroids (perfect labels), known asteroids from catalogues (real labels, positions from JPL Horizons), and everything else called bogus (some label noise). The sky itself is not labelled.

2. What is injection-recovery and why do you add the fake to both science and difference images? Plant objects with known properties, run the normal pipeline, count what comes out. A real asteroid is in tonight's science image but not in the reference; adding the same stamp to science and difference keeps reference = science - difference unchanged, which is exactly that situation.

3. Why draw fake magnitudes uniformly instead of like real asteroids? So every brightness bin has enough fakes to measure completeness. Real asteroids are mostly faint; remember this when quoting rates, since our training mix is not the sky's mix.

4. How do you make the fakes realistic? Use ZTF's own PSF for each frame, sub-pixel rendering with exact flux, Poisson noise, the real frame times, and motion straight on the sky (converted to each frame's pixels). Then test realism with adversarial validation.

5. What is adversarial validation and what did it show? Train a classifier to separate two datasets that should look the same (fakes vs real known asteroids, magnitude-matched, cross-validated by night). AUC about 0.5 means indistinguishable; we got 0.46-0.48 (about 0.47 +/- 0.02).

6. How many chance matches do you expect when matching detections to catalogue positions? P(random point within 2 px of a detection) = detections x pi x 4 / good area = 1.15%. With 114 known asteroids in the field that is about 1.3 chance matches per frame, which is what we saw, so faint "known" matches are mostly wrong. Hence only V <= 20.5 is used.

7. What was wrong with SkyBoT positions and how did you find out? The CNN's most confident "bogus" detections were known asteroids. SkyBoT's predictions sat about 2.9 arcsec (2.7 px) from detections in a constant direction on every night. Our WCS matches Gaia to 0.04 px and JPL Horizons to 0.1-0.3 arcsec, so SkyBoT was off. We switched to Horizons positions (median error 0.26 px vs 2.72 px) with a 2-px radius.

8. Why did you pass the observatory code I41 to the catalogue query? By default positions are for the centre of the Earth; ZTF is about 6,400 km away, which shifts a 1 AU asteroid by up to about 9 arcsec, more than 4 pixels.

9. What is label noise in your "bogus" class? Variable stars, other real transients, and undiscovered asteroids are all labelled bogus. So a "false positive" on real data may be a real object, which is the thing we hunt. On a training night it is a tiny fraction of 36,000 detections, so the model barely notices.

10. Describe the stamp and why three channels. 3 x 31 x 31: science, reference, difference. A real new object shows in science and difference but not reference; a bad subtraction shows a star in both and a dipole in difference; a cosmic ray is a spike narrower than a star. The CNN compares the three panels like a person does.

11. How do you normalise stamps and why? (value - median) / (1.4826 x MAD) per stamp and channel, clipped to [-10, 50]. Median and MAD are robust to a bright star; "5" then means 5 sigma on any night; clipping stops a saturated star dominating. Nothing is fitted on training data, so there is no leakage.

12. Why augment, and why only the training data? Rotations by 0/90/180/270 plus mirror give 8 versions per stamp: the sky has no up, and it acts like more data. Validation and test must measure performance on data as it really is, so they are never augmented.

13. Why balance the training set and what is the cost? With 3% positives the gradient is dominated by easy bogus examples. We use all real + 5 bogus per real (16.7% real). The cost is inflated probabilities (the model thinks real is 16.7% common), fixed afterwards by base-rate adjustment or calibration. Validation is not balanced, so precision and false-alarm numbers stay honest.

14. What went wrong with the first float16 stamps? Saturated star cores exceed 65,504, the largest float16 value, and became inf. Fix: clip before converting. Found by scanning every stamp file after the build, not just trusting the code.

B. Neural network theory

15. Why do you need a non-linear activation? Without it, stacked linear layers collapse into one linear function and depth adds nothing. ReLU makes each hidden neuron a "bent line"; many bent lines give a curved boundary (half-moons: logistic regression 0.88, network 0.94).

16. Explain the forward pass of a one-hidden-layer network. z1 = X W1 + b1, h = ReLU(z1), z2 = h W2 + b2, p = sigmoid(z2).

17. What is the loss and why does a confident wrong answer cost so much? Binary cross-entropy -mean(y log p + (1-y) log(1-p)). At p = 0.01 for a true positive the loss is -log 0.01 = 4.6, while a confident right answer (p = 0.99) costs 0.01.

18. Derive why dL/dz = p - y for sigmoid with cross-entropy. sigma'(z) = sigma(z)(1 - sigma(z)); the derivative of the loss with respect to p is (p - y) / (p(1 - p)); multiplying gives p - y: the sigmoid's slope cancels the log's slope.

19. What is backpropagation? The chain rule applied from the output backwards. Output weights: h^T (p - y) / n. Hidden error: (p - y) W2^T x ReLU'(z1). Hidden weights: X^T (hidden error) / n. Each layer reuses the next layer's result, so one backward sweep gives every gradient.

20. How did you check your hand-written gradients? Numerical gradient: nudge one weight by epsilon = 1e-5 on both sides, (L(w+e) - L(w-e)) / 2e. It matched backprop to 8 decimals (-0.02896520). Removing the ReLU' term makes the check fail at once.

21. What happens with a learning rate of 5.0, and what does Adam do? Gradient descent overshoots and diverges. Adam adapts each weight's step size using its recent gradients; a robust default.

22. Why must initial weights be random? If all weights start equal, every neuron gets the same gradient and stays identical forever.

C. The CNN

23. How many parameters does the first conv layer have? (3 x 3 x 3 + 1) x 16 = 448. In total the network has 72,145.

24. Why do spatial sizes go 31 -> 15 -> 7 -> 3? Padded 3x3 convolutions keep the size; each max-pool halves it (integer division). 64 channels x 3 x 3 = 576 numbers feed the dense layer.

25. What does dropout do? It randomly switches off 30% of the neurons in the head during training so the network cannot rely on any single neuron; it is turned off when scoring (model.eval()).

26. How do you know when to stop training? Validation average precision is checked every epoch; we keep the best epoch and stop after 4 epochs without improvement (the best was epoch 21, AP 0.899).

27. Why judge by validation AP and not training loss? Training loss measures memorising; only held-out nights show generalisation. Training is balanced and augmented, validation is realistic, so their losses are not even comparable (training 0.08 vs validation 0.035 can both be right).

28. Why is the CNN "better" if the completeness curves look alike at 90% recall? Because we fixed recall; the gain is purity: 176 instead of 813 false alarms per frame at the same recall. Purity makes linking cheap: only about 24% of detections remain and seed pairs drop from 52,000 to 3,500.

29. What is cross-fitting? Score each training night with a CNN trained on other nights (4 folds of 2 nights). The tracklet model uses CNN scores as features; scoring nights a CNN trained on gives over-confident scores that would not hold on new nights. Folds are by night, never by detection.

30. What mistakes did the CNN make that taught you something? Its top-scoring "bogus" detections were known asteroids our labels had missed: a label error, not a model error. On the test night, 9 tracklets counted as false positives by the tracklet model were also real known asteroids, so the effective false-positive count was 0.

D. Judgement

31. Is 0.938 AP on the test night impressive? It beats the baseline everywhere, but AP on a set rich in planted fakes flatters; the evaluation chapter uses depth, known asteroids recovered and unexplained alarms per night.

32. What would you change with more time? Human-labelled real/bogus examples to complement injections (as ZTF's own classifier uses), a bigger stamp (63 x 63), a calibration step so scores read as probabilities, and an ablation of the three channels.
